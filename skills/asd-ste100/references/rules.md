# ASD-STE100 - The 53 Writing Rules (Part 1)

This file summarizes the 53 writing rules across 9 sections. Use it during analysis (Step 2 of SKILL.md) and during rewriting (Step 3). Each rule below is paraphrased from ASD-STE100 Issue 9 (January 2025). For the full authoritative text, download the standard free at asd-ste100.org.

The rules differentiate between **procedural writing** (instructions that tell the reader what to do) and **descriptive writing** (explanations of how things work, what happened, or why).

---

## Section 1: Words (Rules 1.1–1.14)

**1.1 - Use approved words, technical nouns, or technical verbs only**
Every word in your text must be one of three types: approved in the STE dictionary, a technical noun (member of one of 22 categories), or a technical verb (member of one of 4 categories). If a word is not in the dictionary, check whether it fits a category before rejecting it.

**1.2 - Use approved words only as the specified part of speech**
Each approved word in the dictionary is listed with exactly one part of speech. Use it only as that part of speech.
- "Test" is an approved *noun* but not an approved verb. Non-STE: "Test the system." STE: "Do a test of the system."
- "Check" is an approved *noun* only. Non-STE: "Check the pressure." STE: "Do a check of the pressure."
- Some words are approved for more than one part of speech ("clean" is both a verb and an adjective). The sentence position shows its function.

**1.3 - Use approved words only with their approved meanings**
Each approved word has a single approved meaning - sometimes narrower than standard English.
- "Follow" approved meaning: "come after, go after." Non-STE: "Follow the safety instructions." STE: "Obey the safety instructions."
- "Fall" approved meaning: "move down by gravity." Not "decrease."
- "About" approved meaning: "concerned with." For the "approximately [a number]" sense, use APPROXIMATELY (adv). For the "around an axis" sense, use AROUND (prep).

**1.4 - Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives**
The dictionary gives verb forms as: infinitive/imperative, simple present, simple past, past participle (as adjective only). For irregular verbs, all forms are listed. Adjectives are given in base form with comparative/superlative in parentheses if irregular.

**1.5 - Technical nouns: use words that fit a technical noun category**
Technical nouns are noun terms that refer to a specified concept in a subject field. There are 22 categories (see `dictionary.md`). The dictionary does not include technical nouns because each subject field uses different ones.

**1.6 - Use a non-approved word only when it is a technical noun or part of one**
Some words not in the dictionary can still be used if they fit a technical noun category.
- "Base" is not approved, but can be a technical noun in geometry ("the base of the triangle") or facilities ("access to the base").
- "Backup" is not approved as a verb, but can be a technical noun in computing ("do the backup").

**1.7 - Do not use technical nouns as verbs**
- "Oil" is a technical noun (category 4). Non-STE: "Oil the surfaces." STE: "Apply oil to the surfaces."
- Exception: some words can be both a technical noun and a technical verb in different contexts (e.g., "drill" can be a tool or an action).

**1.8 - Use technical nouns approved in your company, industry, or subject field**
If your organization has an approved term for a component, use that term. These are usually in your company glossary or terminology database.

**1.9 - Select short, easy-to-understand technical nouns (≤ 3 words)**
When no approved technical noun exists, choose one that is short and clear. Use index numbers and illustrations when possible to keep the noun short.

**1.10 - Do not use regional, slang, or jargon words as technical nouns**
- "Choker" (logging cable, regional North America) → "cable"
- "Brick" (IT slang for making a device non-operational) → "set to OFF"
- "Gear" (jargon for tools and equipment) → "tools and equipment"

**1.11 - Do not use different technical nouns for the same item**
Once you choose a technical noun for an item, use it everywhere. If the approved term is "actuator", do not switch to "servo control unit" or "control unit" in other parts of the text.

**1.12 - Technical verbs: use verbs that fit a technical verb category**
Technical verbs are verb terms for specified concepts or processes. There are 4 categories (see `dictionary.md`). If an approved verb in the dictionary accurately gives the instruction, use that instead of a technical verb.

**1.13 - Do not use technical verbs as nouns**
- "Ream" is a technical verb (manufacturing). Non-STE: "Give the hole a 0.20-inch ream." STE: "Ream the hole to a 0.20-inch dimension."
- The past participle form of a technical verb can be used as an adjective ("the reamed hole").

**1.14 - Use American English spelling**
Use Merriam-Webster spelling. Colour → color, centre → center, organise → organize, fibre → fiber. Use a different spelling only if other directives (contracts, style guides, other official specs) specify otherwise. Quoted text (placards, display text) keeps its original spelling.

## Section 2: Multi-Word Nouns (Rules 2.1–2.2)

**2.1 - Write multi-word nouns of no more than three words**
A multi-word noun is a group of words that function as one noun. Long clusters are hard to parse, especially for non-native readers whose language puts the head noun first. Break longer clusters with prepositions.
- Non-STE (5 words): "Runway light connection resistance calibration"
- STE: "Calibration of the resistance of the runway light connection"
- Non-STE (5 words): "Remove the engine transmission housing attachment bolts"
- STE: "Remove the bolts that attach the transmission housing to the engine"

**2.2 - Long technical nouns: write in full then use a shorter form or hyphens**
When a technical noun from an official document has more than 3 words, write it in full the first time. Then explain it and use a shorter form (≤ 3 words) or an approved abbreviation for the rest of the text.
- Method 1 - shorter form: "ramp service door safety connector pin" (first use) → [explanation] → "safety connector pin" (rest of text)
- Method 2 - hyphens: hyphenated words count as one word. "cutoff-switch power connection" = 3 words. Do not hyphenate unrelated words.

## Section 3: Verbs (Rules 3.1–3.7)

**3.1 - Use only the verb forms given in the dictionary**
Each approved verb in the dictionary lists its forms. Use only those forms.

**3.2 - Use only these verb forms and tenses**
- Infinitive (to install)
- Imperative / command (Install)
- Simple present (installs, install)
- Simple past (installed)
- Simple future (will install)
- Past participle as adjective only (the installed component)

Not allowed: present perfect ("has installed"), past perfect ("had installed"), present/past progressive ("is installing", "was installing"), or any complex auxiliary construction.

Approved auxiliary modals: CAN (to be possible / able to / permitted to) and MUST (requirement). The modals may, might, shall, should, and would are not approved (use CAN or MUST). COULD is a form of CAN but must not be used to show possibility.

**3.3 - Use the past participle form as an adjective**
Past participles show condition. They appear before a noun ("the disassembled unit") or after "to be", "to become", or "to stay" ("the unit is fully disassembled"). This is not passive voice. Some past participles are approved as adjectives even when their verb is not approved ("permitted", "damaged").

**3.4 - Do not use auxiliary verbs to make complex verb constructions**
Do not use auxiliary verbs (be, have, must be, is to be) to make complex verb constructions. Use the approved simple forms. (CAN and MUST are approved; the ban is on complex auxiliary constructions, not on all modals.)
- Non-STE: "The seat is to be installed before you install the cushion."
- STE: "Before you install the cushion, install the seat."
- Non-STE: "The temperature must be adjusted."
- STE: "Adjust the temperature."

**3.5 - Use the -ing form only as a technical noun or modifier**
The -ing form causes ambiguity because it can function as multiple parts of speech. Permitted only as:
- A technical noun: "Cleaning", "Testing", "Handling"
- A modifier within a technical noun: "air-conditioning system", "grinding wheel", "welding torch"
Only a small number of approved words in the dictionary have -ing forms (listed in the dictionary).

**3.6 - Use the active voice. In descriptions, passive is allowed only when the agent is unknown**
- Active: subject does the action ("A does B")
- Passive: subject receives the action ("B is done by A")
- Procedures: active voice always. No passive.
- Descriptions: active preferred. Passive only when the agent (person or thing doing the action) is genuinely unknown.
- Test: can you ask "by whom or by what?" and get an answer? If yes, rewrite to active.

Four methods to convert passive to active:
1. **Move the agent** (object of "by") to subject position. Non-STE: "The circuits are connected by a switching relay." STE: "A switching relay connects the circuits."
2. **Change infinitive verb to active verb.** Non-STE: "These values are used by the computer to calculate the energy consumption." STE: "The computer calculates the energy consumption from these values."
3. **Use imperative.** Non-STE: "The test can be continued by the operator." STE: "Continue the test."
4. **Use "you" or "we".** When the agent is the reader → "you". When the agent is your organization → "we". Non-STE: "On the ground, the valve can be opened with the override handle." STE: "On the ground, you can open the valve with the override handle."

**3.7 - Use an approved verb to describe an action, not a noun**
- Non-STE: "The ohmmeter gives an indication of 450 ohms." STE: "The ohmmeter shows 450 ohms."
- Non-STE: "Before the removal of the unit, make sure the power is OFF." STE: "Before you remove the unit, make sure the power is OFF."

## Section 4: Sentences (Rules 4.1–4.5)

**4.1 - Write short, clear sentences**
Procedures: give short, direct instructions in imperative form. Descriptions: one topic per sentence, no imperative. Be accurate - do not give abstract information that can have multiple meanings.
- Non-STE (abstract): "Different temperatures will change the cure time."
- STE (specific): "When the temperature increases, the cure time will decrease."
- STE (specific): "The cure time is 2 hours at a temperature of 20 °C."

**4.2 - Do not omit words or use contractions**
Every sentence must have all its parts. No contractions (don't → do not, isn't → is not). Do not omit nouns, verbs, subjects, or articles.
- Non-STE (missing subject): "If installed, remove the shims." STE: "If shims are installed, remove them."
- Non-STE (missing article): "Remove the bolt and stop." STE: "Remove the bolt and the stop."

**4.3 - Use a vertical list for complex texts**
When a sentence contains many items or actions, use a vertical list with colons, numbers/letters/bullets, and consistent formatting:
- Put a colon before the first item
- Start each item with an uppercase letter
- Use articles where applicable
- Use periods only at the end of items that are full sentences
- Do not mix procedural and descriptive content in the same list
- Do not nest lists at different levels - flatten them
- Each item must connect clearly to the text before the colon

**4.4 - Use connecting words and phrases between related sentences**
Approved connecting words: and, but, then, thus. Approved connecting phrases: "as a result", "at the same time". In procedures, use connecting words only when an explanation is necessary after a work step.

**4.5 - Use articles before nouns where applicable**
Use "the", "a", "an", "this", "these" before nouns. Do not omit them to shorten text. Do not use articles before general statements or concepts ("Solvents can cause damage to paint."). Do not use "the" before nouns with alphanumeric identifiers ("Tag circuit breaker 36L7", not "Tag the circuit breaker 36L7").

## Section 5: Procedural Writing (Rules 5.1–5.5)

**5.1 - Maximum 20 words per sentence**
Every procedural sentence must be 20 words or fewer. This includes WARNING and CAUTION sentences. Notes (rule 5.5) are an exception: max 25 words.

**5.2 - One instruction per sentence unless actions occur at the same time**
Write one instruction per sentence. Use numbers or letters to show the sequence. Exception: two actions that occur at the same time ("Hold the panel in its open position and install the fastener.") or one action whose result immediately follows ("Make sure the torque is minimum 0.50 Nm. Then, torque each bolt to 4.20 Nm.").

**5.3 - Write instructions in the imperative form**
Use the command form. Do not use "must" before the imperative unless the instruction is very important for safety.
- Non-STE: "Before you remove the clamp, you must disconnect the hose."
- STE: "Before you remove the clamp, disconnect the hose."

**5.4 - Conditions first, then the command, separated by a comma**
When a condition is necessary for a work step, write the condition first, then a comma, then the instruction.
- Non-STE: "Set the switch to NORMAL when the light comes on."
- STE: "When the light comes on, set the switch to NORMAL."
- The comma position matters - it changes which word the adverb modifies.

**5.5 - Write notes only to give information, not instructions**
Notes contain descriptive text and obey descriptive-writing rules (max 25 words per sentence). They must not give instructions, requirements, or limits. Notes can have multiple sentences, each max 25 words.

## Section 6: Descriptive Writing (Rules 6.1–6.6)

Descriptive writing gives information, not instructions. The imperative form is not permitted in descriptive text. Descriptive writing can be: a description of an item, product, system, or component (its function, how it is made, how it operates); a text that gives general information (reports, brochures, papers); or a note in a procedure. Active voice and tense in descriptions are governed by Rule 3.6 and Rule 3.2 - Section 6 does not re-state them.

**6.1 - Give information gradually**
In a descriptive text, give information gradually and make sure each sentence contains only one subject. If you give too much information too quickly, the text is not easy to understand and the reader must read it again.

**6.2 - Use key words and key phrases to give your text a logical structure**
Key words and key phrases connect different ideas across sentences and give the text a logical structure. You can also use approved connecting words ("and", "but", "then", "thus") and connecting phrases ("as a result", "at the same time"). When you select key words and key phrases, do not change them in your text - the same terminology keeps the text clear and correct.

**6.3 - Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 25 words in each sentence**
Descriptive sentences must be 25 words or fewer. If a sentence exceeds this limit, split it.

**6.4 - Use paragraphs to show related information**
Group related information into paragraphs. Start a new paragraph when the information is no longer related to the previous paragraph.

**6.5 - Make sure that each paragraph has only one topic**
Each paragraph has a single topic, introduced by the first (topic) sentence.

**6.6 - Make sure that no paragraph has more than six sentences**
Do not write paragraphs with more than 6 sentences. Split long paragraphs.

## Section 7: Safety Instructions (Rules 7.1–7.3)

Safety instructions tell the reader that a procedure or work step can be dangerous or cause damage. Issue 9 defines **two** safety-instruction levels:

- **WARNING** - risk of injury or death. Must be obeyed.
- **CAUTION** - risk of damage to objects (machines, tools, equipment).

If both risks occur together, use a WARNING. Other industries may use different words or graphical symbols (for example "danger" or "attention"), but the content must still obey Rules 7.1 thru 7.3. **NOTE is not a safety instruction.** A note is a descriptive element governed by Rule 5.5: it gives information, not instructions; it obeys descriptive-writing rules (max 25 words per sentence); and it must not use the imperative form.

**7.1 - Use an applicable word (for example, "warning" or "caution") to identify the level of risk**
Use a word (or, when applicable, a symbol) that immediately shows the reader the level of risk. Do an accurate risk analysis first, then decide: risk of injury or death → WARNING; risk of damage to objects → CAUTION; both → WARNING.

**7.2 - Start a safety instruction with a clear and accurate command or condition**
Your reader must know how to prevent accidents. Start with the direct command, or - if the reader must know a condition first - give the condition first, then the command.

**7.3 - Give an explanation to show the risk or possible result**
Tell the reader what problems can occur if they do not obey the safety instruction. A clear, specified risk makes the person who does the task more careful.

**Word limit for safety sentences:** warnings and cautions obey Rule 5.1 (maximum 20 words per sentence). The standard states this explicitly in Section 5. Notes obey Rule 6.3 (max 25 words) instead, because they are descriptive.

**Legal-controlled text (practice, not a numbered rule):** warning text may be controlled by legal or regulatory departments. When you rewrite text that contains a WARNING or CAUTION, apply STE rules but flag every change for human review. Do not change the original safety meaning. This is a working practice - there is no Rule 7.4 in Issue 9.

## Section 8: Punctuation and Word Count (Rules 8.1–8.7)

**8.1 - You can use all standard English punctuation marks but not the semicolon (;)**
The semicolon lets you write very long sentences and is hard to use correctly. Write two different sentences instead.
- Non-STE: "Examine the removed parts; replace the damaged ones."
- STE: "Examine the removed parts for damage. Replace the damaged part(s)."

**8.2 - Use hyphens (-) to connect words that are directly related**
Use the hyphen for technical nouns to show that two or more words are one unit. Five cases:
1. Two-or-more-word adjectives before a noun: "low-altitude flight", "high-pressure chamber", "quick-release fastener".
2. Two-word fractions or numbers: "forty-seven", "ninety-ninth", "three-sixteenths".
3. An uppercase letter or a number plus a noun that gives a shape or configuration: "L-shaped bracket", "O-ring", "3-prong connector".
4. Verbs that contain a noun or a different part of speech as the first part: "die-cast", "arc-weld", "heat-treat", "short-circuit".
5. A prefix ending in a vowel plus a root word starting in a vowel: "pre-amplifier", "de-icing", "anti-icing".
A hyphen is different from a dash. A dash divides ideas, shows a range, or signals a pause; STE does not give a dedicated dash rule.

**8.3 - You can use parentheses**
Use parentheses:
- To make references to illustrations or text: "Remove the valve (10, Figure 1)."
- To include letters or numbers that identify items: "Disconnect the hoses (2) and (12)."
- To identify the work steps in a procedure: "(1) Install the locking cap (4)."
- To include abbreviations: "Liquid Crystal Display (LCD)".
- To give the singular and plural forms of a noun at the same time: "Before you do the test(s), install the component(s)."
- To explain a word or part of a sentence: "Increase the pressure slowly (not more than 10 psi each minute)."
- To include an alternative: "Open the left (right) access panel."

**8.4 - In a vertical list, a colon (:) has the same effect on word count as a period and shows the end of a sentence**
In a vertical list, the colon divides the first part of the sentence from the items. Thus the text before the colon obeys the normal limit (max 20 words in procedures, 25 in descriptions), and each item after the colon is a new sentence with its own 20/25-word limit. (Vertical-list *structure* is Rule 4.3.)

**8.5 - When you put text in parentheses, it counts as one word in that sentence**
The parenthetical text also forms a new sentence of its own, counted separately. An identifier in parentheses (a number, letter, or alphanumeric identifier) counts as one word; an abbreviation in parentheses counts as one word. Example: "Remove the safety pin (10)." = 5 words.

**8.6 - Count each of these elements as one word**
- Numbers
- Numbers together with units of measurement ("10 °C", "20 kg")
- Abbreviations ("NASA", "VPN")
- Alphanumeric identifiers ("36L7", "No. 1")
- Quoted text (text in quotation marks, uppercase display text, formulas)
- Titles, headings, and text on placards and labels
- Proper nouns of individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities

**8.7 - Hyphenated words count as one word**
A hyphenated term counts as a single word for sentence-length purposes (also see Rule 2.2, which uses hyphens to keep a long technical noun to three "words").

## Section 9: Writing Practices (Rules 9.1–9.4 + General Recommendations)

**9.1 - Use a different sentence construction when a word-for-word replacement is not sufficient**
The dictionary gives approved alternatives for words that are not approved. If an alternative has the same part of speech and does not change the meaning, you can swap it word-for-word. Otherwise, change the sentence construction. Four reasons force a new construction:
1. The grammatical structure must change to use the alternative. Non-STE: "The oil level on the sight gauge must be visible during the test." STE: "During the test, make sure that you can see the oil level on the sight gauge." (approved verb "see" replaces the adjective "visible"; "must be" becomes "make sure that you can".)
2. A word-for-word replacement gives a meaningless result.
3. The approved alternative changes the meaning. Non-STE: "Just apply very light pressure." STE: "Only apply very light pressure." (NOT "Immediately apply..." - "immediately" is the dictionary alternative for "just" but changes the meaning here.)
4. The word is not in the dictionary at all; think of a different approved word or construction that keeps the meaning.

**9.2 - Use each approved word correctly**
Approved words usually have one approved meaning and one approved part of speech. Use them only as listed.
- "wear" (v) means "become damaged by friction", not "have clothing on". Non-STE: "Wear protective clothing." STE: "Use (or put on) protective clothing."
- "extend" (v) means "increase in dimension or range". Non-STE: "This regulation extends to all units." STE: "This regulation is applicable to all units."
- "see" (v) means "see with your eyes", not "come to know". Non-STE: "Move the tube to see if the inner connection is tight." STE: "Move the tube to make sure that the inner connection is tight."
- "turn" (v) means "move around an axis or point". Non-STE: "The indicator turns green." STE: "The color of the indicator changes to green."
- "work" is an approved noun, not a verb. Non-STE: "When you work with cleaning agents..." STE: "When you do work with cleaning agents..."
- "help" is an approved verb, not a noun. Non-STE: "with the help of a second person" STE: "with the aid of a second person".
- "damage" is an approved noun, not a verb. Non-STE: "Be careful not to damage the sleeve." STE: "Be careful not to cause damage to the sleeve."
A small number of words are approved as more than one part of speech (for example "flush" as a verb and as an adjective). The position in the sentence shows the function.

**9.3 - When you use two words together, do not make phrasal verbs**
Do not combine approved words into a new phrase that has a meaning different from its parts.
- Non-STE: "After you put out the fire, close the valve." STE: "After you extinguish the fire, close the valve." ("extinguish" = "to stop burning".)
- Non-STE: "This compound can give off poisonous fumes." STE: "This compound can release poisonous fumes."
Only a small number of phrasal verbs (for example "put on" and "come on") are approved in the dictionary, each with a restricted meaning.

**9.4 - When you select terminology or wording, always use a consistent style**
The same instruction gets the same wording every time it occurs; the same item gets the same noun every time. Do not switch between "Lubricate the bolts with oil" and "Apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the bolts" for the same step. Do not switch between "main body", "body", and "body assembly" for the same item. Different wording for the same thing causes confusion and delays.

### General Recommendations (GR-1 to GR-8)

These are **not STE rules**. They are recommendations that help prevent typical writer errors.

- **GR-1 The conjunction "that".** Use "that" to connect a subordinate clause after verbs such as "make sure", "show", "recommend". It marks where the main clause ends and helps translation. Write "Make sure that the valve is open.", not "Make sure the valve is open."
- **GR-2 The preposition "with".** "With" has three approved meanings (association, help/sharing, means/instrument) and can be ambiguous. Read the sentence again. Write a condition first when needed, and show the primary action verb. Non-STE: "Make sure that the lever does not touch the stop (1) with hydraulic pressure supplied." STE: "When you supply hydraulic pressure, make sure that the lever does not touch the stop (1)."
- **GR-3 Pronouns.** Use only approved pronouns (the dictionary lists them; "he" and "she" are not permitted). If a pronoun can refer to more than one noun, replace it with the word it refers to.
- **GR-4 The pronoun "this".** Make sure the reader knows which item "this" refers to. If "this" can refer to more than one item, give the context again.
- **GR-5 False friends.** A word that looks the same as a word in your native language can have a different meaning in English. Check the English meaning, not the look-alike.
- **GR-6 Latin abbreviations.** Do not use "e.g.", "i.e.", "etc.". Use English words: "for example", "that is", "and so on" (or omit when not necessary).
- **GR-7 Inclusive language.** Use gender-neutral language. "he" and "she" are not permitted. "man" and "woman" are not permitted unless necessary (for example, in a medical text).
- **GR-8 Possessive form.** The possessive ("manufacturer's instructions") is permitted. Use it correctly. If you are not sure the sentence is correct, do not use the possessive form.
